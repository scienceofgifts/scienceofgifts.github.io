As much as we think of giving gifts as an altruistic act of giving, in which *"it’s the thought that counts"*, it is a much more complex system with lots at stake. Humans across different cultures and civilizations have been practicing it in one form or another. This leads anthropologists to believe that gift exchange is intrinsic to human societies and is essential to social harmony. 

Not all gifts are the same. There are rules on what is appropriate and what is considered not. A bad gift can hurt relationships since gifts convey meaning and a bad gift conveys a lack of commitment to the relationship and to the community you’re beholden to.

Research on the subject has given scientists a framework on models of the gift exchange process. Your relationship with your recipient determines which model you are in. The model you’re in, determines what kind of a gift is appropriate, which is why learning about these models is important for anyone trying to become better at picking good gifts.

Following are the three models.  



<h2 itemprop="headline">The Three Relationship Models</h2>
<div class="right-image">
    <img itemprop="image" src="../assets/gift-wrapping-book.jpg" loading="lazy" width="400" height="400" alt="Someone Wrapping a Book"/>
</div>

Looks matter in life. How attractive you look plays a part in how you’re treated by others and how successful you’ll be in your life. The same principle applies to gifts. Aesthetics of a gift shouldn’t be ignored and neither should presentation be. All the time and energy you spend picking the perfect thing for your recipient will fall short if you do.

Humans are not androids. We don’t look at the world in an unbiased, rational view. Our perception of it is filtered through a lens of emotions. All our senses play a part in this perception. 

If you want to make the best out of your gift, you must consider the following requirements that make a gift feel valuable to your recipient. Following are four ways to make sure your gift succeeds in that. 

<h3 itemprop="headline">The Economic Exchange Model</h3>

People you provide for fall into this category. Your children, grandchildren and other kids in your relations. This changes when the kids grow up and your relationship with them matures. 

In this model gifts are exchanged in form of goods and economic factors such as time and money spent are emphasized. 

In this model givers give importance to the value and benefit of the gift itself. The idea of fair exchange is what drives the gift selection. A good gift enhances their own self perception as a provider.  
  

<h3 itemprop="headline">The Relational Partnership Model</h3>


People you have close personal relationships with fall into this model. Spouses, older children, romantic partners, close friends and family members. This model puts an emphasis on companionship and intimacy.

Gifts serve as symbols. Recipients extract meaning from the gifts they receive. The symbols can mean different things. For some they can signal commitment from their partners. Inefficient gifts can be better signals to convey this commitment for both the giver and the recipient. 

>Efficient gifts can backfire for the recipient. In one study a woman expressed how distressed she was when her boyfriend gave her a cappuccino maker as a gift. She worried that the gesture meant that he no longer viewed her as “sexy.” The cappuccino maker, while a useful tool, doesn’t make for a seductive gift.

In intimate relationships, both givers and recipients gauge each others commitment to the relationship by exchanging inefficient gifts. Take, for example, a guy takes a girl for dinner to an expensive restaurant. This expense signals to the girl his willingness to invest in the relationship and her acceptance of this gift signals to the guy that she’s is invested in the relationship and is not with him for monetary gains. The dinner doesn’t increase her wealth.
 

The gifts you give in this model are meant for building and maintaining relationships. 
  


<h3 itemprop="headline">The Community Commitment Model</h3>


People from the community you’re part of fit into this model. It could be family members, co-workers and any other community you’re a part of and identify as a member. 

Researchers had previously ignored this model in their work. Maybe they though it didn’t play much part in the choice that consumers made. But this model is as significant or maybe even more than the previous ones. Communities have rules they abide by when it comes to exchanging gifts with one another. These rules are often unwritten ones and are expressed through social norms. A failure to conform  to these norms leads to communal disruption and a loss of status for the member who is responsible. It also plays an important part in the decision making of the consumers buying the gift, whether they’re aware of these choices or not. 

>A study designed to find a [correlation between the weight of wine bottles and their prices]( https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329312000031 'Link to the Study') found that in most cases the weight of a wine bottle correlated positively with the price. It suggested that consumers can extract potentially useful information by ‘feeling’ the price of the wine. The weight helped consumers perceive the wine as better. 

This model emphasizes social bonds within a community. Gifts that members give reflect the commitment to it They pick gifts that that’ll prove them to be a valuable member of the community. 



<h3 itemprop="headline">How They Work in the Real World</h3>

The stages are dynamic in nature and often determined by the relationship of the giver, recipient and the occasion. You can move from one stage into another as the relationship matures.

> Many large retailers and websites offer gift presentation option on checkout. If you don’t have time to wrap up a gift on your own you can just pay a little extra to get it done.

Presentation expresses special thought on behalf of the giver. When they receive a gift, recipients process the exchange and analyze the giver in terms of thoughtfulness, which is communicated through gift presentation. 



<h3 itemprop="headline">To summerize</h3>

The lesson here seems to be that aesthetics and presentation matter in gift exchanges. Make it look pretty, feel heavy, seem expensive, wrap it up nicely, and your recipient will be happy. If you don’t your time and efforts spent on picking the ideal gift for them won’t be as effective.
